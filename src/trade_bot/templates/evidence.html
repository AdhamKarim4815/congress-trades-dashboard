Does following them actually work?

Every other page here assumes copying members of Congress is worth doing. This page is the test of that assumption — run walk-forward, priced at the disclosure date, with no lookahead. It is the number that should decide whether you trust the board’s buy side. It takes purchases only, and what that leaves untested is set out below.

Is the ranking doing anything, or is it picking three from eighty?

Walk-forward testing stops this measurement seeing the future. It does not stop it getting lucky: three members are chosen from around eighty, twenty-two times over, and some of them will look good by chance alone. The only way to know how much is to run the identical walk while ignoring the ranking — same eligibility bar, same periods, same pricing, members picked at random — and see where the real result lands.

What the measurement could not see

A disclosure whose company has no price series is dropped from every score and every position, silently. That is survivorship, and the shape of it matters more than the size: the companies that vanish are the ones that were acquired, delisted or renamed, and they pile up in the oldest years of the window.

The decay is the tell. What the prices behind these numbers do and do not include

  • Splits are handled. Every cached series is back-adjusted, so a return spanning a 10-for-1 is measured correctly rather than as a 90% loss. That is not taken on trust: the plausibility stage re-scans every series each run for a close-to-close step sitting on a corporate-action ratio, and {% if validation %} the last run found {%- set split_finding = (validation.findings | selectattr('code', 'equalto', 'unadjusted_split') | list) %} {% if split_finding %}{{ split_finding[0].count }} to look at{% else %}none{% endif %}. {% else %}it has not run yet.{% endif %}
  • Dividends are not. Providers return split-adjusted closes, not total return, so every figure here understates income. It biases the result far less than it sounds: excess return is a difference of two unadjusted series, so what is missing is the gap between a holding’s yield and the index’s, not the yield itself. Over a {{ s.horizon_days if s and s.positions else 90 }}-day window with both sides yielding on the order of 1–2% a year, that gap is a few tenths of a percent — material against a small edge, not against the difference between the top and bottom of the ranking. A holding with an unusually high yield is understated by more, and nothing here corrects for it.

How the test avoids flattering itself

  • Entries are at the disclosure price, never the member’s. Members disclose up to 45 days after they trade. The price they got is not a price you could have paid, so the test uses the first close after the filing became public.
  • Rankings use only outcomes known at the time. At each rebalance, members are ranked using trades whose full {{ s.horizon_days if s and s.positions else 90 }}-day window had already closed by that date. A trade disclosed three weeks earlier has not finished yet, so it cannot inform the ranking that selects it.
  • Every position is compared to the same window of the index. A 12% gain in a month when the market rose 14% is a loss, and is counted as one.

What this does not prove

  • Nothing about the sell side. Every position here is a purchase. A disclosure cannot be copied into a short, so there is no honest way to take a member’s sale as a position and measure it — which means the board’s sell cards, and the “at selling” ranking on the members page, rest on the scoring alone. They are ranked by the same direction-signed excess return the buy side uses, and that scoring has never been walk-forward tested in this direction.
  • The sample is small, and now says so in figures. Every result above carries its 95% interval, and several of them contain zero. Where one does, the measurement is not distinguishing the strategy from doing nothing — that is stated on the row rather than left as a general caution here.
  • Whoever happens to be filing shapes the result. Members enter and leave the ranking, and the ones with enough trades to rank are not a random sample of Congress.
  • One window is one window. A strategy that worked across this period is weak evidence about the next one, particularly since it spans a market that mostly rose.
  • Disclosures are ranges, not amounts. Filings report brackets like $1,001–$15,000, so position sizing here is unweighted by conviction.

This is a measurement, not a promise, and nothing here is financial advice.
